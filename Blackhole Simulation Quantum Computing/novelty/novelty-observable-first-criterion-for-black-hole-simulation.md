# Novelty: Observable-first criterion for black-hole simulation

  * Dimensions



Assessed theory: [theory-1](<../theories/operational-equivalence-classes-for-quantum-black-hole-simulation-revised.md>)

### Dimensions

### Phenomenon / Effect — Derivable Unstated

**What is Known:** Across analogue-gravity and holographic/QI toy-model literatures, papers already operationalize “black-hole(-like)” behavior by measuring specific signatures (thermal Hawking/Unruh spectra and correlations, OTOCs/chaos diagnostics, teleportation-through-wormhole correlators, Page-like transitions) under an explicit platform-to-model mapping and within some validity regime. At least some papers explicitly emphasize that a single signature is not a reliable, universal diagnostic (e.g., teleportation-like signals can occur without other holographic/chaotic indicators), motivating multi-observable validation.

**What is Introduced:** The statement elevates this into a general meta-level empirical/operational regularity: a black-hole simulation claim is well-posed only relative to an explicitly specified quadruple (O target observables, M mapping to device degrees of freedom, R validation relation(s) among observables, W validity/universality regime). It adds the explicit underdetermination claim: absent (O,M,R,W), different simulators can match one signature while disagreeing on others, so “we simulated a black hole” has insufficient content.

**What is Novel:** The underlying ingredients (choosing observables, providing a mapping, checking some relations, and discussing regime-of-validity) are largely already present as practice in many papers, and the caution about single-signature insufficiency is explicit in at least some. What appears novel in aggregate is the explicit formalization into a portable, prescriptive criterion/checklist (O,M,R,W) and the framing of “black-hole simulation” as an operational equivalence-class claim rather than a single phenomenon; this looks more like a synthesized principle than an established, named standard in the sampled literature.

### Explanatory / Mechanistic — Derivable Unstated

**What is Known:** Across analogue-gravity and holographic/quantum-information simulation papers, it is already standard (often implicit) to provide a concrete model-to-device mapping and to validate at least one targeted signature via a quantitative relation (e.g., Hawking/Unruh detailed-balance thermality; OTOC-based scrambling diagnostics; teleportation/decoding fidelity bounds). Several works also discuss, in domain-specific contexts, additional resource requirements for stronger information-theoretic claims (e.g., needing U and U*, postselection/amplification) and note ensemble/typicality caveats when comparing gravity path-integral results to single-instance Hamiltonians.

**What is Introduced:** The statement introduces an explicit, general operational prescription for when a “black-hole simulation” claim is well-posed: specify (O) a target observable set, (M) an explicit mapping making M(O) measurable, (R) at least one inter-observable validation relation to be preserved, and (W) a validity/universality regime. It also elevates scattered operational considerations into a unified explanatory rule that different experiments can be legitimately inequivalent (matching one signature but not others) and that stronger claims demand additional capabilities and clarified ensemble-vs-instance assumptions.

**What is Novel:** The aggregated evidence suggests the underlying physical mechanisms for particular signatures are not new (and in some cases are explicitly established), while the novelty lies in packaging existing practice into a compact, device-agnostic criterion (O,M,R,W) for claim well-posedness plus an explicit capability/assumption checklist for claim strength. Most surveyed papers either exemplify parts of (O,M,R,W) for a specific platform/model or discuss resources/ensemble issues piecemeal, but do not state this as a general necessary-condition rule; thus the statement is best viewed as a synthesizing mechanistic/operational abstraction rather than a new physical mechanism.

### Unification — Derivable Unstated

**What is Known:** Across the assessed papers, strong unification already exists within specific paradigms: analogue/QFT-in-curved-spacetime works provide explicit device mappings for horizon/QFT phenomena, and holography/quantum-information works unify scrambling, OTOCs, teleportation, and decoding diagnostics. Reviews/white papers and surveys often juxtapose analogue-gravity experiments with SYK/JT/teleportation/decoding programs and note they probe different signatures, but they typically do not formalize a field-level criterion that makes a “black-hole simulation” claim well-posed in an operationally comparable way.

**What is Introduced:** The statement introduces an explicit, general unifying taxonomy: a black-hole-simulation claim is only well-posed relative to an operational specification (O,M,R,W), and different experiments should be understood as belonging to potentially inequivalent operational equivalence classes defined by those choices. It also explicitly highlights a coarse but practical bifurcation (kinematic fixed-background QFT analogues vs holographic/quantum-information analogues) to explain why disparate demonstrations can be simultaneously legitimate yet not mutually validating the same “black-hole physics.”

**What is Novel:** The aggregated evidence indicates that the explicit (O,M,R,W) equivalence-class framing and the normative, cross-paradigm classification principle are generally not stated as such in the assessed literature; rather, they are at best inferable by synthesizing across disparate reviews, surveys, and platform-specific papers. One NISQ traversable-wormhole/SYK paper comes closest by integrating multiple diagnostics and validation checks into an operational workflow, but it still stops short of articulating the broader equivalence-class principle as a general organizing framework for all black-hole simulation claims.

### Generalization / Scope Expansion — Derivable Unstated

**What is Known:** Across analogue-gravity/QFT-in-curved-spacetime simulations and holographic/QI toy-model simulations, papers typically define a platform-specific mapping from some target model to device degrees of freedom and then validate one or a few signatures (e.g., Hawking/Unruh spectra, OTOCs/chaos, teleportation/decoding metrics), often with an implicit regime-of-validity. Reviews and community overviews already juxtapose many modalities and signatures, but they generally do not state a single, modality-independent criterion that must be satisfied for the claim “we simulated a black hole” to be well-posed.

**What is Introduced:** The statement introduces an explicit, modality-agnostic operational scoping requirement for black-hole-simulation claims: specify (O) the target observable set, (M) the mapping to measurable device quantities, (R) at least one validation relation among observables expected to be preserved, and (W) the regime/universality window where R should hold. It further asserts this applies uniformly across both major classes of “black-hole simulation” (kinematic analogue QFT mappings and holographic/quantum-information analogues) and elevates ensemble-vs-single-instance/typicality assumptions as part of what must be specified when comparing to gravity calculations.

**What is Novel:** Aggregated evidence suggests many ingredients are commonplace at the case-study level (papers already have some M and some checked observables), but the explicit elevation to a single prescriptive cross-modal standard (O,M,R,W) for what counts as a well-posed simulation claim is usually not stated as such. Some assessed papers support treating this as a synthesis/abstraction that follows naturally from comparing disparate signatures across subfields (i.e., “derivable but unstated”), while several others—especially narrow single-platform demonstrations or purely theoretical gravity papers—contain no comparable platform-spanning operational prescription, making the explicit cross-modal criterion appear genuinely new relative to those works. Overall, the best aggregate characterization is that the statement is a novel scope-expanding operational generalization: largely derivable from the literature in hindsight, but not established as an explicit community-wide criterion in the sampled papers.

### Constraint / Limitation — Derivable Unstated

**What is Known:** Across analogue-gravity and holography/quantum-information “black-hole simulation” literatures, papers routinely state platform-specific limitations (e.g., coordinate/boundary artifacts, finite-size/discretization, finite-N deviations, noise, resource demands like TFD preparation and U/U* access) and often caution that reproducing one signature (e.g., thermality or scrambling) is not sufficient to establish stronger claims. Several works also explicitly discuss averaging/typicality issues in technical form (e.g., Haar-averaging requirements; ensemble/replica-wormhole interpretations; code-subspace/state-dependence constraints), but typically inside their own problem setting rather than as a general criterion for simulator claims.

**What is Introduced:** The statement turns these scattered caveats into a prescriptive necessary-condition constraint on simulation claims: a “black-hole physics” simulation is well-posed only if it specifies (O) a target observable set, (M) an explicit mapping making M(O) measurable on the device, (R) at least one validation relation among observables in O that should be preserved under M, and (W) the regime where R is expected to hold. It further asserts that without (O,M,R,W) the claim is logically underdetermined because different simulators can match a single signature while disagreeing on others, and it elevates ensemble-vs-single-instance comparability (and associated typicality/code-subspace assumptions) to an explicit requirement when gravitational theory outputs are ensemble-averaged.

**What is Novel:** The aggregated evidence suggests the underlying limitations motivating caution are largely not new individually (many are explicit and, in reviews/white papers, fairly established), but the explicit “(O,M,R,W) checklist or the claim is underdetermined” principle is generally not stated as a named, cross-platform necessary-condition criterion in the surveyed papers. In some papers the ingredients appear in partial or peripheral form (e.g., noting insufficiency of single diagnostics; mentioning averaging/factorization caveats), so the novelty is best characterized as an abstraction/synthesis that makes implicit methodological requirements explicit and operational, rather than a newly discovered failure regime. Overall, this is strongest as a formalization that is mostly derivable from prior cautions, with pockets where it would read as genuinely new to narrower experimental papers that lack such meta-level criteria.

### Conceptual Reframing / Abstraction — Derivable Unstated

**What is Known:** Across analogue-gravity and holographic/quantum-information simulation papers, it is already common practice to (i) pick specific target diagnostics/observables, (ii) provide an explicit model-to-device mapping (e.g., discretization, effective-metric mapping, Jordan–Wigner), and (iii) validate by checking one or more expected relations or bounds within an assumed regime. At least one paper suggests this observable-oriented stance exists in the community as an (informal) methodology, but not as a named, prescriptive criterion for when a “black-hole simulation” claim is well-posed.

**What is Introduced:** The statement elevates this scattered practice into an explicit, normative meta-criterion: a claim of “simulating black-hole physics” is well-posed only relative to an operational specification (O, M, R, W), where R includes at least one relation among observables expected to be preserved under the mapping within a validity window. It thereby reframes simulation claims as membership in an operational equivalence class and asserts underdetermination when only a single signature is matched.

**What is Novel:** Aggregated evidence indicates the novelty is primarily the explicit abstraction/checklist (O,M,R,W) as the unit for stating, comparing, and adjudicating simulation claims across otherwise incommensurate platforms, rather than any new physical effect or diagnostic. However, multiple papers contain enough ingredients that this criterion is plausibly reconstructible as a synthesis (derivable-but-unstated), and one paper indicates components may be explicitly present but peripheral in experimental-holography practice; thus the contribution is best characterized as a novel formalization/standardization of an implicit norm, not a wholly unprecedented idea.

### Empirical Synthesis / Meta-Regularity — Derivable Unstated

**What is Known:** Across the assessed corpus—especially reviews/white papers—there is already a broad, explicit juxtaposition of multiple “black-hole-like” targets (Hawking/Unruh thermality, scrambling/OTOCs and level statistics, teleportation/wormhole and Hayden–Preskill-style decoding diagnostics, Page-curve/island ideas), and some sources explicitly discuss ensemble-averaged gravity calculations versus single-instance Hamiltonian realizations. However, these discussions are typically not presented as a single field-level rule for when a “black-hole simulation” claim is well-posed, nor as a standardized operational checklist that must accompany such claims.

**What is Introduced:** The statement elevates a cross-literature meta-regularity into an explicit criterion: a “black-hole simulation” claim is underdetermined unless it specifies an observable set O, a device mapping M making M(O) measurable, at least one validation relation R among observables expected to be preserved, and a regime W where that preservation is intended. It also systematizes the idea that multiple inequivalent simulator classes can each legitimately claim “black-hole simulation” (e.g., kinematic fixed-background QFT analogues vs holographic/quantum-information analogues), and makes ensemble-vs-single-instance comparability (typicality/averaging and code-subspace restrictions) part of the required specification rather than an optional interpretive caveat.

**What is Novel:** Relative to the assessed papers, the novelty lies primarily in codifying a prescriptive, observable-first comparison protocol (O,M,R,W) plus an explicit “single-signature is insufficient” underdetermination claim as a synthesized conclusion meant to apply across platforms and model families. The least-novel evidence comes from high-level syntheses (notably the Snowmass white paper) that already discuss many constituent ingredients (multiple targets; interpretational issues including ensemble vs single-instance), making the contribution best characterized as a stronger, centralized formulation and partially new synthesis rather than a wholly unprecedented discovery. Overall, the corpus supports that the statement is largely inferable from prior discussions but not commonly stated as an explicit, standard criterion.
